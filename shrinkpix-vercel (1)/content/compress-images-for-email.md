You attach a handful of holiday photos, press send and receive a bounce message saying the attachment is too large. It is a familiar frustration, and it happens because modern phones and cameras produce files far bigger than email was designed for. The good news is that shrinking pictures for email takes only a minute and rarely changes how they look on a screen.

## Why email has size limits

Most email providers cap a single message at around 20 to 25 MB, including everything attached. Attachments are also encoded for transport, which adds roughly a third to their size, so a 15 MB set of photos may behave like 20 MB. Corporate servers, older systems and recipients on mobile data often have stricter limits, so smaller is always safer. A message under 5 MB is delivered reliably almost anywhere.

## Understand what makes photos heavy

A current smartphone photo is commonly 3 to 8 MB with dimensions of 4000 pixels or more. Yet a laptop screen shows roughly 1500 to 1900 pixels across, and a phone screen even fewer. The extra pixels are invisible to the recipient unless they plan to print the image. That is why resizing is the single most effective step.

## Step one: resize the dimensions

Reduce the width to between 1200 and 1600 pixels for photos that will only be viewed on screen. This alone can cut file size by seventy to ninety percent. Most operating systems include a basic resize option in their photo viewer or sharing menu, and online tools can handle batches. Keep the aspect ratio locked so nothing looks stretched.

## Step two: compress the file

After resizing, run the pictures through a compressor. For photographs saved as JPG, a quality between 70 and 80 percent is usually indistinguishable from the original on screen. Upload up to ten images to ShrinkPix, choose your quality, compare the before and after previews and download the results. If the preview shows visible blockiness, raise the quality a little and compress again from your original file, not from the already compressed copy.

## Choose the right format

Photographs belong in JPG. Screenshots and documents with sharp text look better as PNG, though they are larger. If a PNG screenshot is still too heavy, try compressing it or cropping away empty margins. Avoid sending uncommon formats that the recipient may not be able to open, such as raw camera files or HEIC images from some phones, unless you know they can.

## Combine several images wisely

Sending many pictures in one message increases the risk of hitting the limit. Compress them all first, then check the total size. If the total is still above 10 MB, split them across two emails or use a download link instead. Creating a ZIP of already compressed JPG files saves very little, because they are already compressed, but it is convenient for keeping a set together.

## When to use a link instead

For very large collections, original quality prints or video, an attachment is the wrong tool. Upload the files to a cloud storage service and share a link. This keeps your message light, lets the recipient choose when to download and avoids rejection by strict mail servers. Remember to set sensible sharing permissions.

## Protect your privacy

Photos can contain hidden metadata such as the date, camera model and sometimes GPS location. Compressing and resizing often removes much of it, but if you are sharing publicly or with strangers, check your settings or strip metadata deliberately. Never send sensitive documents as unprotected images; password protect them or use a secure service.

## Keep your originals

Compression for email is a one-way trip. Always save your original files before shrinking anything, because you cannot restore lost detail later. Work on copies, name them clearly, and keep the originals in a safe folder or cloud backup.

## Quick checklist

Resize to about 1600 pixels wide, compress at 70 to 80 percent quality, confirm the total is under 10 MB, and preview the result. For very large sets, share a link. Follow these steps and your messages will arrive quickly, open easily and look great on any screen.

## Sending from a phone

Most email apps on phones offer to resize attachments for you, usually with options such as small, medium and large. Medium is generally enough for viewing on screens, while large keeps more detail for printing. If you want precise control, compress the pictures first and then attach them from your gallery or files. Be careful on mobile data, since uploading large photos can use up your allowance quickly.

## Frequently asked questions

How many photos can I send at once? As long as the total stays under the limit, any number works, but keeping a message below about 10 MB is safest. Will the recipient notice the lower quality? Rarely, if the images are resized to around 1600 pixels and compressed at moderate quality. Should I zip the photos? It helps keep them together, but it does not shrink JPG files much, so compress them first. What about scanned documents? Save them as JPG or PDF at a sensible resolution, since 150 to 200 dots per inch is generally readable and much lighter than very high scan settings.
