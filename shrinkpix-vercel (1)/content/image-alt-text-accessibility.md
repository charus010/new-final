Alt text is a short written description attached to an image. For people who cannot see the picture, it is the only way to understand what it shows. It also helps search engines interpret your images and appears when a picture fails to load. Writing it well is a small habit that makes your content more inclusive and more discoverable.

## What alt text is and who needs it

The alt attribute sits inside the image tag in your HTML. Screen readers read its content aloud to people who are blind or have low vision. Others benefit too: visitors on slow connections who see the text while a file loads, people who disable images to save data, and anyone whose browser fails to fetch the file. Search engines use it as one signal of what the image contains.

## The golden rule: describe the purpose

Good alt text answers the question of why the image is there. The same photo of a laptop might need different descriptions depending on context. In a product review, it could say a silver 14 inch laptop with the lid open showing a code editor. In an article about remote work, it could simply say a person typing on a laptop at a kitchen table. Think about what information a visitor would miss without the picture.

## Keep it concise and natural

Most alt text works best at a single sentence or less, commonly under 125 characters. Write the way you would describe the image to a friend on the phone. Skip phrases like image of or picture of, because screen readers already announce that the element is an image. Avoid repeating the caption word for word, since that makes users hear the same thing twice.

## Do not stuff keywords

Search engines are good at spotting spammy text. An alt attribute such as cheap shoes buy shoes online best shoes sale helps nobody and can harm your site's credibility. Include a relevant keyword only when it truly describes the image, and let it appear naturally. Accurate description is both the accessible choice and the SEO-friendly one.

## Handle decorative images correctly

Some images carry no information, such as divider lines, background flourishes and purely stylistic icons. Give them an empty alt attribute, written as alt with nothing between the quotes. Screen readers then skip them entirely. Leaving the attribute out altogether is different and may cause the file name to be read aloud, which is confusing.

## Images that contain text

If a picture includes text, such as a banner or infographic, the alt text should include that wording. For long or complex material, summarise the key message in the alt attribute and provide the full data nearby in normal page text or a table. Better still, avoid placing important text inside images, because it cannot be resized, translated or searched.

## Charts, diagrams and screenshots

For a chart, state the main conclusion, for example a line chart showing monthly visitors doubling between January and June. For a diagram, describe the structure and steps briefly. For a screenshot, say which screen it shows and what the reader should notice. Put detailed data in text below the image so that everyone can use it.

## Logos and linked images

A logo that links to the home page should have alt text that describes the destination or the brand, such as ShrinkPix home. When an image is the only content of a link or button, the alt text acts as the link label, so it must say what will happen when activated. A magnifying glass icon should read search, not magnifying glass.

## Check your work

Use a screen reader such as the one built into your phone or computer, or install a browser extension that lists missing alt attributes. Search your site for images without descriptions and fix the most important ones first. Make alt text part of your publishing checklist alongside compressing the file and choosing a clear file name.

## Conclusion

Strong alt text is accurate, brief and written for people first. It opens your content to a wider audience, satisfies accessibility guidelines and gives search engines useful context. Add it to every meaningful image you publish, mark decorative ones as empty, and review older pages when you can. The effort is small, and the benefits last.

## Examples of good and weak alt text

A weak description is simply dog. A better one is a brown spaniel catching a yellow frisbee on a beach. For a product page, a weak version is shoes, while a helpful one is white leather running shoes with a grey sole, seen from the side. For a chart, avoid graph and write sales rising from 10,000 to 40,000 units over four quarters. These examples show how small additions of colour, action and context turn a label into a real description.
